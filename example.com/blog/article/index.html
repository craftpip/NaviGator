The Year I Stopped Clicking "Accept"

By Maya Chen · Aug 10, 2026 · 12 min read · Filed under essays, web-culture, privacy

MC
Maya Chen writes about technology, cities, and the small rituals of daily life. Her work has appeared in the Testville Herald, AJDS, and nowhere else of note. She can be reached at maya@chendev.example.

It started as a small rebellion. I was on a cooking website, trying to read a recipe for potato soup, and the page demanded that I choose between "Accept all" and "Manage preferences" before I could see a single ingredient. So I clicked neither. I closed the tab instead.

The soup went unmade. But something in me switched.

The deal we all made

Somewhere along the way, consent became a reflex. Every site asks, and every one of us clicks the big green button without reading, because reading would take four minutes and we just want the recipe, the weather, the bus schedule.

The EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) was supposed to fix this. Instead, it created an entirely new category of UX failure: the consent banner that does not actually consent. The ones where "Reject all" is grey, tiny, or buried under three menus, while "Accept all" is a neon billboard.

As researcher Elena Márquez put it:

"Dark patterns in consent dialogs are not a bug — they are the product. Every additional click in the 'manage preferences' flow loses 40% of users. Companies know this. The UI is designed to make refusal as frictionful as possible."

— Elena Márquez, The Architecture of Online Consent (2024), p. 47

So for one year, I decided to refuse. Not by tweaking settings — but by simply never clicking accept anywhere. If a site blocked me, I left. If it made me choose, I chose the most restrictive option available. If it asked twice, I stopped visiting.

My methodology

I tracked every interaction in a simple CSV file with the following columns:

date,site,action,result,notes
2025-08-01,recipe-blog.example,refused,blocked,"potato soup lost"
2025-08-03,weather.example,refused,accessible,"no banner required"
2025-08-04,news-site.example,refused,partial,"could read 2 paragraphs"
2025-08-05,tractor-forum.example,refused,blocked,"10-post minimum to lurk"

After 12 months, the data told a clear story. Here's a summary of what I found:

Key findings

73% of sites
accepted the refusal without blocking content. The banner closed and the page was readable. These are the good actors.
18% of sites
showed a degraded experience — fewer images, disabled comments, or a persistent "update your preferences" bar. Functional but annoying.
6% of sites
completely blocked access until consent was given. These sites treated the entire page as a hostage negotiation.
3% of sites
had no consent banner at all. Government sites, small personal blogs, and Wikipedia.
Note
The 73% figure is for all sites visited, not just the 152 that presented a consent dialog. The remaining 38 had no banner.

What I lost

I lost access to a regional news site, two recipe blogs, and a forum about vintage tractors. That last one hurt more than I expected.

The tractor forum — TractorTalk — requires a 10-post minimum before you can even view threads without a consent banner. I could see the titles, but not the content. The posts were about restoring a 1967 ATM-1200, and I wanted to read the whole thread. I even considered caving.

"The real cost of refusing consent is not the content you lose — it's the communities you can't join. And those communities are exactly where the most passionate, niche knowledge lives."

This is what I wrote in my journal on Day 47. I still think about those tractor posts.

What I gained

I gained a much clearer picture of which sites actually respect you. The best ones let you read first and ask later. The worst ones treat the entire page as a hostage negotiation.

Here's what I learned, broken down by category:

Recipe sites

Are, on average, the worst offenders. The pattern is always the same:

  1. A massive hero image of the dish
  2. The author's life story (3–5 paragraphs of SEO content)
  3. A "Jump to recipe" button that also requires consent
  4. The actual recipe, buried under three more ads

The recipe itself is about 200 words. The page is 4,000.

Government sites

Are surprisingly polite. Most have no banner at all. The ones that do use a simple "This site uses cookies for analytics" with a single dismiss button. No dark patterns, no forced choices, no "manage preferences" labyrinth.

News sites

Are the most interesting case. Major outlets split into two camps:

ApproachExamplesUX qualityPrivacy impact
Accept or leave DailyChronicle, TimesTest Poor — blocks readers Good — clear refusal
Accept to read, ask later Most regional papers Medium — at least readable Medium — defaults to accept
No banner at all Public broadcasters, Wikipedia Excellent Excellent
Accept all, reject hidden Major commercial outlets Poor — dark pattern Poor — defaults to full tracking

Nobody reads the cookie text

This is the deepest irony. The entire apparatus — the banners, the preference centers, the legal teams, the UX designers — exists for a consent that almost nobody gives meaningfully. A 2023 study by the Testville Institute for Digital Rights found that only 2.3% of users ever opened the "manage preferences" panel. Of those, 89% clicked "Accept all" anyway.

But everybody would rather not be asked.

The economics of refusal

There is a real cost to refusing. Not just the content, but the time. Every refusal takes 5–15 seconds. If you visit 40 sites a day, that's 3–10 minutes of pure friction. Over a year, I spent approximately t = Σ(ti) ≈ 2,400 minutes — or 40 hours — just closing tabs and finding alternatives.

Forty hours. That's almost two full workdays spent on the act of refusal.

A note on methodology
The 2,400-minute figure includes time spent finding alternative sites, not just closing tabs. The pure "close tab" time was closer to 800 minutes (13 hours). The rest was navigation overhead.

But here's the thing: the sites that survived my refusal were almost always better. Faster. Cleaner. More respectful of my time and attention. The 40 hours of friction saved me hundreds of hours of cookie-banner-induced distraction.

The technical deep dive

For the technically inclined, here's what actually happens when you refuse consent. Most sites use one of three frameworks:

OneTrust
Sets a OptanonAlertBoxClosed cookie. When set to a past timestamp, the banner re-appears on next visit. Refusal is treated as "you haven't decided yet."
Cookiebot
Sets CookieConsent with a JSON payload: {stamp:'...',necessary:true,preferences:false,statistics:false,marketing:false}. Clean, readable, and actually respects refusal.
Custom implementations
Wildly inconsistent. Some set no cookies at all (good). Others set tracking cookies anyway and show the banner for compliance theatre (bad). One site I tested set 14 cookies before the consent dialog even loaded.

The technical community has a term for this: compliance theatre — the appearance of following privacy law without actually doing so. The banner exists to satisfy auditors, not users.

What a good implementation looks like

A well-designed consent flow has these properties:

  • Default to refuse — all optional cookies off until the user explicitly enables them
  • Equal visual weight — "Accept" and "Reject" buttons are the same size, color, and position
  • No pre-ticked boxes — every category starts unchecked
  • One-click dismiss — closing the banner counts as refusal, not deferment
  • No second ask — once refused, the banner does not reappear for at least 6 months

Of the 152 consent dialogs I encountered, exactly 4 met all five criteria.

The verdict

A year in, my browser is clean, my reading is calmer, and I have strong opinions about a potato soup I never made. Next year: browser notifications. I'm not ready, but neither are they.

If you want to try this yourself, here is my advice:

  1. Start with one week, not a year. See how it feels.
  2. Keep a log. The data makes it interesting instead of frustrating.
  3. Find your "tractor forum" — the one site you'll actually miss. That tells you what you value online.
  4. Don't beat yourself up. I caved twice (once for a recipe, once for a news article behind a hard wall). The goal is awareness, not purity.

"Consent should be a conversation, not a checkbox. But right now, it's mostly a checkbox — and the fine print is written in CSS."

— Maya Chen, in a talk at TDC 2026
